# System Cartography

System Cartography treats software architecture as a carefully surveyed terrain rather than a stack of boxes. Space carries the explanation: user-facing surfaces sit at the left edge, orchestration forms the central landmass, retrieval engines occupy the technical right edge, and evaluation/data preparation orbit as calibrated instruments. The composition should feel meticulously crafted, as if every connection was measured, redrawn, and refined by a systems designer at the top of their field.

Form is modular and precise. Rounded rectangles behave like engineered components; thin connector lines behave like signal paths; grouped regions behave like strata in a technical map. The final image should communicate through adjacency, layering, and directional flow more than prose. Text remains sparse, clinical, and essential-only: labels, short capability phrases, and version markers are visual anchors, not explanations.

Color is a restrained information system. Cool blues and violets express agentic orchestration, green expresses user and authentication surfaces, amber expresses evaluation and metrics, red-pink expresses fallback/risk boundaries, and deep slate gives the whole artifact technical gravity. Each color choice must look painstakingly calibrated, not decorative; the work should appear labored over with care and master-level execution.

Rhythm comes from repeated component cards, evenly spaced routes, and deliberate asymmetry. The most important structure is the central agentic workflow, which should visually command the page without overcrowding it. Retrieval and metadata filtering should feel like a refined instrument panel, not a paragraph of features. The result must reward sustained viewing: every small label, line break, and alignment should feel intentional.

The diagram should be polished enough for a GitHub landing page and precise enough for a technical interview. It must look like countless hours were spent balancing hierarchy, density, whitespace, and routing clarity. Nothing should overlap, drift, or feel autogenerated; every shape and line should show painstaking attention and expert craftsmanship.
